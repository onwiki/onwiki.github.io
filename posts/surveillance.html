The Normalisation of Surveillance Tech

Published on 2026-08-08 at 13:57 UTC+12 by Oscar N.
The latest trends in the "Surveillance Aesthetic"

The Earlier Fear

Between the years of 1946 and 1948, a man wrote a book about mass surveillance of the public. This book featured a character designed to be shoved down the throats of the people, in order to help put the public at ease about the idea of being stalked by the government - to reframe the situation. A government watching your every move? A little more than suspicious. A Big Brother watching out for you though - protective, caring, trustworthy - makes a lot more sense.

That book is called 1984, written by George Orwell. I have three copies sitting on my bookshelf, one a nice hardcover that I keep for myself, and the other two are ones I have accumulated through the years and that I lend to people who are interested in reading it.

Orwell's 1984 depicts a world in which its citizens are subjected to constant surveillance, manipulation, and control by an authoritarian government. He wrote of a society where even an independent thought was seen as a potential threat. What it appears 1984 has become more accurate of though, is that no one is going to care enough to stop that world from taking place because we are all being influenced to believe that it's normal.

The Content We Consume

I first noticed this phenomenon - the normalization of surveillance - first years ago with social media. What better way to track a persons thoughts and ideas than to literally have a system where they input their own opinions, interests, and ambitions every day. When you take a second to observe the concept of social media outside of your usual complicitness, you realize just how odd it is. You are fed an algorithm that actively learns from your inputs and interactions with it, so that it can show you things you will more enjoy consuming. It's like an Ouroboros (an ancient symbol of (usually) a snake eating its own tail), where it is it's own demise. By using social media you are actively giving it more reason to keep you attached. This sets the scene for the owners of social media services to have full control of your free time, your 'feed', and what you believe others are doing more so than not. It's all up to them, completely aside for the data they collect from you. 

Now, on the topic of social media, I think it's a good segue to begin talking about the first of many attempts to normalize surveillance: Alexa Chung (a very fitting first name). 

Alexa Chung is a British model, fashion designer, writer, and television presenter, and if you haven't yet picked up on her influence she also boasts over six million followers on Instagram. Recently she, with her influence, posted a collection of photos of her in different outfits - the pictures taken by her Ring doorbell (a "smart" door bell that is always recording and sending that data to it's parent company, Amazon. Heard of them?). The pictures were portrayed as candid, random, and unobtrusive - the very romanticization of a piece of surveillance technology. 

This post was then spread by Who What Wear? a leading UK digital fashion and beauty destination with international reach, serving millions of global readers through trend reports, shopping guides, and celebrity style coverage. Their take on Chung's post: "Did Alexa Chung just make a doorbell cam the coolest new way to do a fit check?" (posted on their official Instagram).

This isn't the only case of a celebrity endorsing surveillance tech in some way, with Kylie Jenner partnering with Meta (parent company of Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp, Meta-Quest, and more) to advertise their new Meta Glasses - designed to be recording using mini cameras on the glasses themselves. These glasses have been under heat with them becoming so normalized by social media influences using them for stunts - and perverts using them to record and replay content typically of women and kids. All this recorded video footage is once again, like the Ring doorbell, sent to it's parent company Meta (owned by Mark Zuckerberg), which has had software code uncovered for the Meta AI app, revealing an unreleased, internally tested facial recognition and "faceprint" tracking system code-named "NameTag". What the hell?

The issue here is that the average person can not compete against a team of specialized people, funded by billionaires, specifically for the purpose of analyzing trends and data and working out how to make these appealing for people to buy. This ties back into the conversation before about social media, because the very same company that is actively collecting data on what EVERYONE likes and dislikes, is in the business of selling the idea of their surveillance goggles.  As well-regarded journalist Elizabeth Whitehead said, "the approval of the fashion world gives people the social permission to wear them," and Meta is partnering with leaders in the fashion industry to ensure we civilians have the psychological 'permission' we need to accept these.

This same approach applied to the latest Spider-Man movie released just 31 July 2026. I love Spider-man, but after the movie was over I was glad I wasn't the only one who picked up on the not-so-subtle use of city-wide facial recognition systems in the movie. In the newest movie, Spider-Man Brand New Day, which achieved the highest domestic opening weekend in box office HISTORY, we see Peter Parker access all the cameras in New York using AI facial recognition software, mimicking the real life equivalent of this right now from companies like Flock. If you don't know what Flock is, the idea is that it is SUPPOSED to be a simple license plate reading camera that uses AI to capture data from what it records. There are about 120,000 Flock cameras across the United States, with other countries implementing their own systems. What is so bad about them? 

The people in control of the Flock system are misusing their power with at least 50 officers already charged with misusing the systems. Many have been using them to stalk their exes, target minorities, and there have even been cases of Flock employees accessing cameras inside of a children's gymnastics facility. This included footage of the children's swimming pool, play grounds, and other private facilities. Tell me, would a good company let this happen repeatedly? The issue with Spider-Man using it isn't what he is using the cameras for, it's that it normalizes the idea of us being watched at all times by someone we don't know, much like the idea of Big Brother in 1994.

The Decisions We Make

The grand belief behind those subjecting us to surveillance isn't just to monitor and influence us, but it is to then further monitor our decisions to see if what they are doing is actually working. We are already seeing this in the masses with Digital ID schemes being rolled out in many countries, forcing users to input a form of their ID into the operating systems of their devices in order for corporations/governments to monitor what they are doing digitally at all times. 

Possibly one of the most prominent aspects of doing anything is what we buy. This isn't new, but it is worth noting, that we have had surveillance of our expenses for a while now, with credit/debit cards, EFTPOS terminals, and digital bank accounts. A mere arrangement of pixels shown as being a convenience, in exchange for our purchasing autonomy. When you pay with cash, your odds of being caught buying something related to what could upset the people/corporations in power is a maybe. Low odds if you play your cards right, but when you pay using a standard digital payment service, everything is recorded including the location and reference, in a complete history. As if this wasn't already reinforced in itself, EFTPOS terminals (starting in New Zealand, but then again that's where fully integrated EFTPOS systems started out too, and look how they've grown) are now going to try ditch the old physical-card in exchange for your face. Facial recognition will now be implemented in stores across an entire country, meaning that your physical appearance will be at the mercy of those writing the terms and condition for the digital payment systems we all use.

The popular video game Watch Dogs 2 warned us. In the game's world, “Appliances, consoles and home security systems give corporations a window into your private life", "Insurance companies use algorithms to monitor your life habits and limit or deny coverage.” and “Search results and news feeds are skewed to bias mood and influence your vote”. This game, released 10 years ago now, is an accurate representation of today's world. As Watch Dogs stated, "you are now less valuable than the data you produce." 

I've heard many people say that if you have nothing to hide then why worry? To that my reply is usually an analogy: if you aren't doing anything illegal in a bathroom stall why do you still close the door? It's human to want ourselves to remain ours alone.  Maybe we should stop asking whether we have anything to hide and start asking who keeps trying to look at us from under the door.
